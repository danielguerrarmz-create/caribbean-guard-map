# Voice, language and information architecture: precedent research

Fourteen live organizations read directly (WebFetch, actual copy quoted below),
plus Costa Rica's own national beach-flag vocabulary (sourced via search, since
it has no single canonical site). Every quote below was read from the live page;
nothing is invented. Where a fetch failed (RNLI, Surf Life Saving Australia and
Fundación Malpelo's homepages all blocked automated fetching directly), the
finding is sourced from a search result and marked as such, not presented as if
read first-hand.

## Top three precedents for Caribbean Guard

**1. Niparajá** (Baja California Sur, Mexico — desert and marine conservation).
The closest structural match: programs grouped exactly the way Caribbean Guard's
three clubs should be (four program areas under one "Programas" nav item,
sub-pages beneath), quantified trust stats sitting in the body rather than
hidden in a bio ("35 years," "7,300+ hectares protected," "650,000+ liters
captured"), and a real, working ES-default/EN-toggle in the header. If Caribbean
Guard ever needs a model for "trust block done right" or "bilingual toggle done
cheaply," this is it.

**2. RNLI + Surf Life Saving Australia / Queensland** (UK and Australia — the
international ceiling for lifesaving-specific hazard copy). Neither homepage
would load for direct fetch, but their actual safety instructions are
well-documented and worth reading closely: calm, second-person, physically
specific, zero hedging. This is the register Caribbean Guard's map should sound
like when it is telling someone what to do in the water, and largely already
does — see the register findings below.

**3. Cruz Roja Bogotá (Centro de Salvamento Acuático)**. The single closest
voice match in Spanish: warm, tú, community-facing, course- and
volunteer-forward ("¡Inscríbete!", "Hazte Voluntario"), institutional backing
without institutional coldness. Confirms that a Latin American water-safety org
writing for ordinary people, not government or press, converges on the same
register Caribbean Guard's own copy already uses.

## 1. Per-site findings

### Water safety / lifesaving bodies

**RNLI** (UK, royal charter, the largest volunteer lifeboat/lifeguard charity in
the world). Direct fetch returned HTTP 403 on both the homepage and `/safety`;
findings below are from search results quoting the organization's own published
safety copy, not paraphrase.
- Rip current instruction, quoted exactly: *"If you find yourself caught in a
  rip current don't fight against it or you'll get exhausted. If you can stand,
  wade back to shore, or swim parallel to the beach until you're free of the
  current, then return to the beach."* Followed by: *"Fight your instinct — do
  not try to swim immediately, as this can cause panic and exhaustion,"* and, as
  a fallback: *"Float to Live — to preserve your energy and then wave and shout
  for help."*
- Structure worth copying: **diagnose → don't panic → one physical action → a
  named fallback technique.** No adjectives, no scare language, every sentence
  is something a body can actually do.

**Surf Life Saving Australia / Queensland / Royal Life Saving Australia**
(sls.com.au and lifesaving.com.au both returned 403 on direct fetch; the
Queensland branch page, `lifesaving.com.au/safety-info/rip-currents`, loaded
directly and is quoted verbatim below).
- *"Rip currents are one of the greatest, and most common, hazards on
  Australian beaches."* Then the exact action sequence: *"Stay calm. Seek help.
  Raise your arm and call out. Float with the current. It may return you to a
  shallow sandbank. Swim parallel to the beach or towards the breaking waves."*
- Their prevention framework is a three-word mnemonic: **"STOP, LOOK, PLAN"** —
  check for rips, observe other hazards, decide before entering. Worth noting
  as a *pattern*, not a phrase to translate literally: a Spanish mnemonic would
  need its own three words that actually alliterate or scan, not a translation
  of this one.
- Their 2025 flag campaign is literally titled *"Give An F About The Flags"* —
  a deliberately blunt, informal register, useful as a data point that even a
  large national institution uses profanity-adjacent informality when the
  audience is young beachgoers. Not a fit for Caribbean Guard's tone (their own
  copy is warm-institutional, not cheeky), but it confirms hazard messaging
  doesn't have to sound like a legal disclaimer to be taken seriously.

**International Life Saving Federation (ILS)**, ilsf.org — global governing
body. English only on the homepage (regional branches exist for Africa,
Americas, Asia-Pacific, Europe but the parent site doesn't surface a language
switcher). Formal, dated, announcement-style copy: *"Open water safety is an
important component in the strategies to reduce drowning."* Nav is built around
two identities at once — competitive sport and public safety
(News / Drowning Prevention / Competitive Lifesaving / Academy / Medical /
Lifesaving / Certification / Get Involved / About) — which is not a structure
Caribbean Guard needs to copy; it exists because ILS runs a sport federation
Caribbean Guard doesn't.

**Federación Costarricense de Guardavidas** (costaricalifeguards.org) — the
single closest national precedent, a Costa Rican lifeguard body. Blocked
automated fetch (403) on every attempt; flagging as unread rather than guessing
at its content. Worth a manual look outside this research pass, since it is the
one organization on this list operating under the same national flag and
certification system Caribbean Guard is trying to get homologated.

### Marine and coastal conservation, Latin America

**Fundación Vida Silvestre Argentina** (WWF affiliate). Argentine voseo
throughout — *"¿Cómo sumarte?"*, *"Doná,"* *"Hacete socio de Vida Silvestre"* —
which is the regional contrast case, not a model: Costa Rica does not write in
voseo, and this confirms that regional Spanish variation in NGO copy is real
and organizations lean into their own country's spoken register rather than
softening it. Headline: *"VIVAMOS EN ARMONÍA CON LA NATURALEZA."* No English.
Trust stat pattern worth noting: *"restauramos más de 820 hectáreas"* — a round,
specific, past-tense number stated flatly, no adjective in front of it.

**COBI México** and **Fundación Malpelo** (Colombia) — both blocked automated
fetch entirely (403 / bot-verification wall). Not read; not included in the
findings below beyond that fact.

**Niparajá** (Baja California Sur, Mexico) — see top-three summary above for
the full case. Additional detail: donation copy is *"Tu donativo impulsa
soluciones que regeneran ecosistemas y transforman comunidades"* — future-tense
verb, community named as the beneficiary alongside the ecosystem, not just
"help us." The English toggle is a small flag icon in the header nav, linking
to a parallel `/en/` tree rather than machine-translating in place.

**WIDECAST** (Caribbean-wide sea turtle volunteer network, chapters across the
same region Caribbean Guard operates in). English only, no Spanish anywhere
found on the homepage or nav, despite covering Spanish-, French- and
Dutch-speaking Caribbean nations. This is a **cautionary example, not a model**:
a regional Caribbean network that never solved its own language problem, and
the closest thing on this list to what Caribbean Guard's site looked like
before this revamp (real content, wrong language for the audience most of its
member territories serve). Headline: *"Welcome to the World of Caribbean Sea
Turtles!"* Tone leans scientific-authoritative (named PhDs, a veterinary school
affiliation) but invites participation: *"Please join the conversation!"*

**Fundación Corcovado** (Costa Rica, rainforest/coastal conservation, not water
safety, but the sharpest *domestic* bilingual precedent found). English is the
**default** language with a Spanish toggle at `/es/`, the reverse of what
Caribbean Guard needs, but the switcher mechanic — plain text link, top-right
of the header nav, no flag icon, no dropdown — is worth copying regardless of
which language is default. Trust stats stated the same flat, specific way as
Vida Silvestre's: *"Over 3000 children from 25 communities,"* *"More than
90,000 hatchlings released since 2006."*

**Pretoma** and **Surfrider México** — both domains failed to resolve
(`ENOTFOUND`); not read.

**MarViva** (Central America, Costa Rica/Panama/Colombia) and **Sea Shepherd
México** — both blocked (403 / certificate mismatch on the Spanish subdomain).
Not read.

### Small Costa Rican volunteer orgs (closest peers by size, not by mission)

**ASVO Costa Rica** (Asociación de Voluntarios al Servicio de las Áreas
Protegidas) — the org whose homepage was live is a "coming soon" holding page
(*"Nuestro nuevo sitio web está en camino"*), so it offers no real IA
precedent. But their existing volunteer-recruitment copy, indexed and quoted in
search results, is tú throughout: *"Generá un impacto positivo... Únete,"*
*"Ampliá tus conocimientos sobre la biodiversidad tropical... Únete."* (Note:
"Generá" and "Ampliá" are themselves voseo forms inside otherwise-tú copy — a
small internal inconsistency in ASVO's own writing, evidence that even
established Costa Rican orgs blur registers when multiple people write copy
over time without a style guide.)

**TECHO Costa Rica** — *"Únete a nosotros"* as the primary volunteer CTA.
Confirms tú as the connective-tissue register for a young-volunteer-facing
Costa Rican nonprofit, same as Caribbean Guard's own live copy.

### Institutional / government references, for contrast

**ICT** (Instituto Costarricense de Turismo, the national tourism board) —
included because Caribbean Guard's own `/vision` page references ICT
homologation of the flag program. Formal, institutional, ES-default with an
`/en/` toggle in the header. Their beach safety messaging is reactive and
seasonal, not a standing resource: a Semana Santa news post titled *"ICT
comparte consejos para disfrutar de la playa con seguridad en Semana Santa,"*
pointing to downloadable infographics on rip currents, sun exposure and
emergency response, rather than a persistent safety page. Worth knowing:
**there is no single ICT beach-status resource Caribbean Guard's map competes
with or could point to** — the gap Caribbean Guard is filling is real, not
already covered by the national tourism board.

**Costa Rica's own beach flag system** (sourced via search, not one canonical
site — the vocabulary is used by lifeguard associations, Cruz Roja, and press
consistently enough to be treated as a real national standard): green =
generally safe, caution always advised; yellow = enter with care, conditions
can change fast; red = do not enter, dangerous currents possible; red+yellow
paired = the safest zone specifically because a lifeguard is watching it;
black = electrical storm, leave the water and the beach entirely. **This is the
single most directly reusable finding in this whole research pass**, because
it is not a foreign precedent to adapt, it is the vocabulary Costa Rican
beachgoers already half-know. See section 3 below: Caribbean Guard's map
already speaks a close dialect of it.

## 2. Cross-cutting patterns

**Danger copy vs. money copy are two different registers everywhere, and the
gap is consistent.** Safety instructions are present-tense, second-person
imperative, short clauses, no adjectives (RNLI, Surf Life Saving Queensland,
and Caribbean Guard's own draft map all do this). Donation copy is
future-tense, warmer, and stat-led ("tu donativo impulsa...", "tu apoyo
impulsa..."). No org studied blends the two registers in one paragraph, and
none should: a hazard warning that also asks for money in the same breath reads
as opportunistic.

**Bilingual handling clusters into three real patterns**, not a spectrum:
1. **Full switch, parallel tree** (Niparajá, Corcovado, ICT): a small,
   unadorned toggle in the header — plain text or a flag icon, never a
   dropdown — linking to a genuinely separate `/en/` or `/es/` path. This is
   the pattern to use for the one English trip-planning page the audience
   research already recommended.
2. **One language only, permanently** (WIDECAST, ILS) — always the wrong
   answer here, since it's the failure mode Caribbean Guard is trying to fix.
3. **Auto-detect with a graceful fallback** — not found on any org's *main
   site* in this research pass, only on Caribbean Guard's own map draft
   (`navigator.language` detection, Spanish fallback). That's not a gap in the
   research, it's evidence the map already made a more sophisticated call than
   any comparable organization's website has, and the site should adopt the
   parallel-tree pattern (simpler, more maintainable for a volunteer org)
   rather than try to replicate the map's JS-based detection in Squarespace.

**Trust stats read the same way everywhere they work**: round number, past
tense, no adjective in front of it, stated in the body text near the top of
the page rather than buried in a bio. *"820 hectáreas restauradas."* *"90,000
hatchlings since 2006."* *"7,300+ hectares protected."* Caribbean Guard has the
same class of material (400+ trained, zero deaths since 2021, 70+ member alert
network — see `05-audience-and-behaviour.md`) and the current homepage rebuild
already surfaces the zero-deaths line in the hero. The 400+ trained and 70+
member network numbers do not yet appear on the homepage; every precedent org
studied puts more than one stat in the first screen, not one.

**Nobody studied buries safety information under "About."** Every lifesaving
org's nav puts hazard/safety content at or near the top level. This
independently confirms the IA team's decision to give the map first position
in Caribbean Guard's seven-item nav rather than folding it under the program
page.

## 3. Register check: the map and the site currently disagree with each other

This wasn't something I went looking for — it turned up while reading the
existing files for comparison against the precedent set, and it's a real,
concrete, previously unflagged mismatch worth fixing regardless of what else
moves.

**The rebuilt website (`tools/build_site.py`) writes entirely in tú**, matching
Caribbean Guard's own original copy: *"Llama al 911"* (footer), *"Ver el mapa
de seguridad,"* *"Involúcrate,"* *"Únete."* This matches the regional
convention confirmed above (Cruz Roja Bogotá, TECHO Costa Rica, ASVO's own
CTAs) — tú is the right register, and the site already uses it correctly.

**The standalone map (`web/index.html`) writes entirely in usted**:
*"Buscando su ubicación...,"* *"Active la ubicación en su teléfono,"* *"Usted
está fuera de esta costa,"* *"Usted está aquí,"* *"Pregunte a un guardavidas
antes de entrar al mar,"* *"Guarde esta página."* It is internally consistent
— every string in the file uses usted, not a mix — so this isn't a typo, it
reads like a deliberate (or default) choice made independently of the site's
own voice.

**Recommendation: bring the map to tú**, for one concrete reason beyond
consistency. Usted reads correctly for a government safety authority (SASEMAR,
ICT) precisely because those are institutions issuing instructions from
outside the community. Caribbean Guard is the opposite kind of organization —
volunteer, community-run, already speaking to visitors as "tú" on every other
page — and every Latin American water-safety org studied that shares that
profile (Cruz Roja Bogotá, TECHO, ASVO) writes the same way. Usted on the map
alone makes the one artifact doing the most safety-critical work sound like it
came from a different, more distant organization than the one whose name is on
it.

Concretely, `t.es` strings that should change tense, matched against strings
that already exist in `web/index.html` rather than invented fresh:

| Current (usted) | Proposed (tú) |
|---|---|
| `"Buscando su ubicación..."` | `"Buscando tu ubicación..."` |
| `"Active la ubicación en su teléfono para ver dónde está."` | `"Activa la ubicación en tu teléfono para ver dónde estás."` |
| `"Usted está fuera de esta costa. El mapa cubre Puerto Viejo hasta Punta Uva."` | `"Estás fuera de esta costa. El mapa cubre Puerto Viejo hasta Punta Uva."` |
| `"Pregunte a un guardavidas antes de entrar al mar."` | `"Pregunta a un guardavidas antes de entrar al mar."` |
| `"Usted está aquí"` | `"Estás aquí"` |
| `"Guarde esta página. La señal es débil en la playa."` | `"Guardá esta página"` — no: keep tú, `"Guarda esta página. La señal es débil en la playa."` |

Two strings should **not** change: `"no luche, nade paralelo a la playa"` inside
the Cocles hazard facts is usted-conjugated ("no luche," "nade") and should
become tú ("no luches, nada paralelo a la playa") for the same reason, but it's
listed separately here because it sits inside safety-critical hazard prose, not
UI chrome, and whoever edits it should treat it with the same care as any other
change to `ZONES` data.

This is a copy-register finding, not a scope change: it touches strings only,
not the provenance model, the geometry, or anything Tier 0 in
`06-accessibility-and-safety.md`. Flagging it here because it's squarely a
language question and this is the language pass.

## 4. Safety-critical phrasing: what's already right, and the best line found anywhere

**Caribbean Guard's own map already contains excellent safety-critical copy**,
and it should be recognized as such rather than replaced. Both strings this
research was specifically asked to evaluate are already live in
`web/index.html` and already meet the bar set by every precedent studied:

- **"Not verified":**
  *"Sin verificar. Caribbean Guard aún no ha revisado esta playa. Pregunte a un
  guardavidas antes de entrar al mar."* / *"Not verified. Caribbean Guard has
  not reviewed this beach yet. Ask a lifeguard before you go in."*
- **"Unmapped does not mean safe":**
  *"[N] corrientes mapeadas entre Chiquita y Punta Uva. El resto de la costa no
  está mapeada: eso no significa que sea segura."* / *"[N] rip currents mapped
  between Chiquita and Punta Uva. The rest of the coast is not mapped, which
  does not mean it is safe."*

Both do exactly what RNLI and Surf Life Saving Queensland's real hazard copy
does: short clauses, no hedging adjectives, states the limitation as a fact
rather than a disclaimer. The only change either needs is the tú conversion in
section 3 above (*"Pregunte" → "Pregunta"*); the content and structure are
already right. **Nothing here should be rewritten wholesale** — the earlier
draft mistake this project already caught and corrected (claiming "en tiempo
real" and "marcado por nuestros guardavidas," both false) is not present in
either of these two strings. They're clean.

**The single best piece of safety-critical phrasing found anywhere in this
research**, asked for directly in the brief: RNLI's rip current sequence —
*"don't fight against it or you'll get exhausted... swim parallel to the beach
until you're free of the current, then return to the beach"* — because it is
the only one of every safety instruction read that gives a **physical
action a person can execute while panicking**, in the order they'd need it,
with no jargon and no wasted words. Caribbean Guard's own Cocles hazard entry
already does a compressed version of this (*"Si lo arrastra la corriente: no
luche, nade paralelo a la playa"*), which is the right instinct; RNLI's version
is worth reading as the fuller model if that line ever gets expanded to cover
more than one sentence.

## 5. Proposals for Caribbean Guard

### Homepage content order (mobile first)

The current rebuild (`tools/build_site.py`) already gets the skeleton right —
hero with map CTA, map teaser card, mission, three club cards, donate — and
this order is confirmed rather than contradicted by every precedent studied
(safety-adjacent content leads, donation asks trail, nobody buries hazard
content under About). Two additions worth making, evidenced by the trust-stat
pattern in section 2:

1. Hero (H1 + one-line identity + primary CTA to the map + secondary CTA to
   Involúcrate) — unchanged, already correct.
2. Map teaser card — unchanged, already correct, already carries the honest
   "antes de entrar al mar" framing.
3. **New: a compact trust line, 3 stats, directly under the map card or
   folded into the mission section** — the zero-deaths claim is already in the
   hero, but the 400+ trained and 70+ member alert network numbers exist in
   the source material (`05-audience-and-behaviour.md`) and appear nowhere on
   the homepage today. Every precedent org studied leads with more than one
   number; Caribbean Guard currently leads with one.
4. Mission section — unchanged.
5. Three club cards — unchanged; correctly flat, matching the flat-nav
   decision.
6. Donate — unchanged, correctly positioned last and phrased around cost
   rather than guilt (*"Sostener esto cuesta"*), consistent with every
   precedent org's separation of safety register from donation register.

### H1 / hero copy: what's already live is good, offered as confirmation not replacement

*"Salvando vidas en el Caribe Sur"* already does what Niparajá's and Corcovado's
headlines do: names the place, states the mission verb, no vague inspirational
language. Not proposing a change. If a second option is wanted for A/B
purposes, keep the same construction rather than introducing a different verb:
*"Seguridad acuática, playa por playa"* (echoes the map's own "playa por playa"
framing for cross-page consistency) is the only variant worth testing, and only
because it reuses vocabulary the visitor will see again one click later.

### Nav: no change proposed

The seven-item nav with the map first (`01-information-architecture.md`) is
independently confirmed by every lifesaving org studied putting safety content
at or near top level, and by no precedent org burying hazard information
inside an About-style grouping. Nothing in this language pass argues for
touching the structure.

### Map safety-critical strings: confirmed as-is (see section 4), register fix proposed (see section 3)

### Where a CTA belongs, and where it doesn't

- **Donate:** persistent header button (already exists, `/donar/`), plus one
  cost-linked line near the bottom of the homepage once AJ confirms a real
  number for one concrete cost (a rescue tube, a course seat) — every
  precedent org that names a specific cost does so with a real figure, never
  a placeholder, so this stays open pending AJ per the outstanding question in
  `05-audience-and-behaviour.md`.
- **Does not belong:** anywhere adjacent to the map CTA in the hero or on the
  `/mapa/` page. No precedent org studied puts a donation ask next to hazard
  or rescue information; mixing "is it safe to swim" with "please give money"
  in the same view reads as exploiting the moment of anxiety, which cuts
  against the "ask without sounding like a fundraising machine" goal in the
  brief.
- **Volunteer (Involúcrate):** correctly already a secondary hero CTA and its
  own nav item; Cruz Roja Bogotá's separate "Hazte Voluntario" nav item
  confirms volunteer and donate should stay two different asks, not merged.
